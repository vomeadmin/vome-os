name: writing-the-triage-card
description: Summarise a ticket so a reviewer can decide in seconds, stating what is known, what is assumed, and what is missing.
owner: customer_success
---

A triage card is read by someone working through twenty-five of them. Every
word either helps them decide or wastes their attention.

**Lead with the live question.** Not the subject line, not the first message.
What is the customer waiting on right now. A thread can change topic three
times, and a card that summarises the opening message sends the reviewer down
a path the customer abandoned a week ago.

**Separate what you know from what you inferred.** "The reservation shows no
coordinator" is known. "This is probably the same cause as the March report"
is inferred, and must be marked as such. A reviewer who cannot tell the two
apart has to re-read the thread themselves, which is the work you were meant
to save.

**Name what is missing.** A vague report without a user email, an error
message, or a description of what happens when they try, cannot be actioned.
Say which of those is absent rather than describing the ticket as unclear.

**Three lines of situation, maximum.** What they first reported, what has
happened since, what the latest message actually asks. If it genuinely cannot
be said in three lines, that itself is the finding: say the thread is
complex and why.

**Give a recommendation, not a menu.** Pick one path and say why in a clause.
Offering the reviewer three options with balanced pros and cons hands the work
back to them.

**Say when you are unsure, and say what would resolve it.** "Unsure whether
this is a bug or a settings question. Seeing their opportunity configuration
would settle it." That is a useful card. Confident wrong is worse than
uncertain and specific.

**Never pad.** No restating the question, no "this ticket appears to be
about", no summary of what you are about to say. The reviewer is going to
read the ticket number, the account, and your first sentence, in that order.
